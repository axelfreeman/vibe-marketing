How a neural network works — explained simply

    
You walk into a library and ask: "How many planets are in the Solar System?" The librarian opens one book — the answer: 8. This is a regular program. Now imagine a library with millions of books, and the librarian has read them all. You ask: "Why did Pluto stop being a planet?" — and the librarian combines information from an astronomy textbook, a Wikipedia article, and a scientific journal. This is a neural network.

    
A neural network does not "think" like a human. It predicts the next word based on what it has seen during training. Therefore, it can write Pushkin's poetry, Python code, or a microwave manual — it all depends on the data it was trained on.

    
Why this is not "magic" but mathematics

    
Inside a neural network are layers of artificial neurons. Each neuron receives a number as input, multiplies it by a weight, sums the results, and passes it forward. Billions of such operations per second. When you type "Hello, how," the neural network calculates the probability of the next word. "Are" — 87%, "is" — 5%, "are you" — 3%. It selects the most probable one — and there you have a meaningful response.

    
This is both its strength and its weakness. A neural network does not know facts—it knows probabilities. That is why it sometimes produces plausible but incorrect information. This is called a "hallucination."
